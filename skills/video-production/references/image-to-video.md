# Image-to-video: practical review

Read this when sourcing or generating moving shots from photographs.

1. Choose images for their role in the story and the intended viewing context. Inspect the full source and the landscape/portrait crop before generating. For a recognizable location, verify the place and obtain the right to use and adapt that exact image; keep geographic and historical context with the source record. If a user wants to approve photos first, present actual previews and wait for selection.
2. Describe composition and style in the source image; describe the motion separately: subject action, ambient motion, camera move, direction, speed, and duration. Specify what must remain still, such as building geometry, lettering, vehicle shapes, horizon, shoreline, signs, or rail geometry. Use a small controlled test before committing a large allocation; change one variable when iterating. Maintain consistent subjects, light, palette, and camera logic across consecutive shots.
3. Inspect returned dimensions and actual duration rather than assuming the requested format was honored. Generated vertical output may crop roofs, statues, signs, or other important content in a landscape film. Frame the actual subject intentionally; find a better source or choose an appropriate layout if the crop cannot preserve it.
4. Review start, middle, end, and real-time playback. Watch for invented text, bending facades, drifting background details, changes in people or objects, floating camera motion, repeated patterns, and temporal flicker. Subtle leaf, cloud, water, light, or fabric movement can work better than forcing entire buildings to move. A controlled still or real licensed footage is better than a visibly warped animation.
5. Edit the source to fit the spoken idea and its timing. Use varied shots or natural transitions rather than repeated loops, jump backs, stuttering photo pans, or freeze-padding. Verify the caption and text-safe area after reframing. If the user wants alternatives, show real image candidates with links and truthful location/rights status, not just verbal descriptions.

Example failure: in a Jefferson film, a 2014 General Store photo animated the bunting but also warped storefront lettering; museum and fountain outputs arrived portrait despite a landscape request. Retain that lesson as a visual test, not as a universal ban on animation. A historic non-local rail image behind text about a particular crossing could imply it depicted that crossing; seek verified local imagery or choose a visual that does not make that implication.
